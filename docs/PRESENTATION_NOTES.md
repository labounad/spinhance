Benchtop spectrometers are cheaper (tens of thousands of dollars compared to 2M+ dollar instruments) and have smaller magnetic fields, so they run at lower frequencies (90 MHz). However, a major drawback is that NMR spectra at these frequencies do not follow first-order splitting and are less human interpretable - we often cannot directly "read off" shifts and coupling constants from "blurry" peak groups - leading to the common misconception that these spectra carry less information than high field spectra. Despite this perception, according to physics, low-field spectra encode the same shift and coupling data as their high-field counterparts, and it is theoretically possible to extract this information. We set out to do just that - to automatically extract shift and coupling data from 90 MHz NMR data. These data fully characterize a molecule's spin system, allowing us to simulate an NMR spectrum for the molecule at arbitrary field strength. 

  We scoped the problem in order to deliver something tractable for this hackathon, limiting ourselves to molecules with exactly 8 spin groups so that we did not have node count variability. Then, we developed a three-stage pipeline in order to aggregate a large dataset:
  1. First, molecules were screened from molecular databases. Initially, we chose ChEMBL - a manually curated database of 2.9M bioactive molecules with drug-like properties. We developed code to identify the structure of the molecules' spin systems from a 3D force-field embedding and classify protons based on chemical and magnetic equivalence. From this we extracted 64K unique molecules with exactly 8 spin groups. 
  Later, we harvested the much larger PubChem database, which contains 123M molecules, and produced 3.1M molecules with exactly 8 spin groups.
  2. To estimate the shifts and coupling constants, we developed a heuristically-driven pipeline to estimate shifts and coupling constants. Critically, we hypothesized that the model did not need accurate NMR data - it just needed a representative sample of physically and chemically plausible spin systems; i.e., spin systems that "look" like they could have come from a real molecule. The heuristics were developed from nmrdb2, an open-source nmr database, and the reference Structure Determination of Organic Compounds: Tables of Spectral Data by Pretsch, Buhlmann and Badertscher. Protons were given estimated shifts and coupling constants (2J, 3J and 4J) based on substructure matching, and small gaussian random variables were used to regularize the data. Karplus equations were also used for 3J couplings. 
  3. Finally, we built a physics spin simulator "pyspin" to convert the spin system data into actual spectra to be used as input training data to our model. We accomplished this through an understanding of the underlying spin mechanics that govern NMR: spectra record energy transitions between distinct nuclear spin states, and the energy of spin states are the eigenvalues of the nuclear spin Hamiltonian, an operator with two terms. The first term is the field-dependent Zeeman term with the shift parameters \sigma_i, and the second term considers spin-spin coupling and is parameterized by the coupling constants J_ij. The eigenvalues of this operator are the energies of the eigenstates, and transition rules determine which energy gaps are probed by NMR pulse sequences. The "stick" spectrum of discrete transitions can be convolved with a fixed-width Lorentzian to obtain the spectrum, simulating the physical acquisition process within a spectrometer and broadening out the peaks.
  A system of N spin 1/2 nuclei yields a 2^N-dimensional spin space, so it becomes computationally intractable to diagonalize the Hamiltonian and solve for each energy. However, pyspin implements a clever clustering strategy and local approximate bases to solve spin systems with linear scaling (as opposed to exponential scaling) and nearly perfect accuracy. This allows us to convert spin systems to spectra at a rate of 1500 per second.
