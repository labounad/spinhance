Spinhance
Reading molecular structure out of a cheap, blurry NMR spectrum.

A 90 MHz benchtop spectrum looks uninterpretable — but physics says it encodes the same chemical shifts and couplings as a $1M magnet. We train a model to recover those parameters, fully characterizing a molecule's spin system from low-field data.

The low-field problem

Low field scrambles the signal — it doesn't erase it.

As the field drops, peak separation Δν shrinks toward the coupling J; neighbouring multiplets overlap and merge into complex, second-order patterns. The same δ and J are still encoded — drag 90→600 MHz and watch the same molecule resolve.

Why fix at 8 groups: a spin system is a graph (nodes = groups, edges = couplings); fixing the node count at 8 means the model always emits the same-shape output — no variable graph size to handle. Then the three-stage pipeline builds data: (1) screen databases for 8-group molecules, (2) estimate plausible δ and J by heuristics, (3) simulate exact spectra with pyspin. Stage 2 · estimate shifts & couplings

The model needs plausible spin systems — not measured ones.

heuristics fill in each node's δ (ppm) and each edge's J (Hz) — the same molecule's complete spin system

The hypothesis: to learn the inverse map, the model only needs spin systems that look like they came from a real molecule — not literature-accurate NMR.

  • δ and J (²J, ³J, ⁴J) by substructure matching against reference tables (nmrdb2, Pretsch et al.).
  • Karplus equation for ³J — dihedral-angle dependence from the 3D geometry.
  • Small Gaussian jitter for variety — not memorized constants.
Payoff: a near-free generator — millions of realistic spin systems, no quantum chemistry per molecule.
Yiming. The bet is that plausible beats accurate-but-scarce for training the inverse map — the model just needs to see realistic spin systems. We fill the graph in by substructure-matching δ and J (²J/³J/⁴J) against reference tables (nmrdb2 and the Pretsch Tables of Spectral Data), use the Karplus equation for ³J from the 3D dihedral angles, and add small Gaussian jitter for diversity. The same molecule's graph from Stage 1 now carries δ on every node and J on every edge (note the two real coupling networks — the methoxyphenyl ring B·C·D·H and the nitro ring E·F·G; the OCH₃ singlet A is uncoupled) — done with no per-molecule quantum chemistry, so it scales to millions.
Stage 3 · pyspin — the physics engine

Spin systems → exact spectra, from first principles.

$$\hat H \;=\; \underbrace{-\,\gamma \sum_i (1-\sigma_i)\,\mathbf B\cdot\hat{\mathbf I}_i}_{\text{Zeeman (shifts)}} \;-\; \underbrace{\sum_{i<j} J_{ij}\,\hat{\mathbf I}_i\!\cdot\!\hat{\mathbf I}_j}_{\text{coupling}}$$
  • NMR records energy transitions between spin states — the energies are the eigenvalues of this Hamiltonian.
  • Selection rules pick the allowed gaps → a stick spectrum, convolved with a Lorentzian to mimic the spectrometer.
  • N spin-½ nuclei → a 2N space — exact diagonalization is intractable. pyspin uses spin-cluster decomposition + local bases → linear scaling, near-exact accuracy.
1500 / sec
spin systems → spectra (open-source engine)
eigenvalues → allowed transitions → stick spectrum → broadened ¹H trace
Lucas. The spin Hamiltonian has two terms: a field-dependent Zeeman term carrying the chemical shifts, and a coupling term in J. Its eigenvalues are the spin-state energies; allowed transitions (selection rules) give a stick spectrum, which we convolve with a fixed-width Lorentzian to mimic the spectrometer lineshape. The catch: N spin-½ nuclei live in a 2^N-dimensional Hilbert space, so exact diagonalization blows up. pyspin decomposes the system into coupled spin clusters with local approximate bases — linear scaling, near-exact accuracy, ~1500 spectra/second, open source.

Sam. The key idea: an unordered graph (8 nodes, 28 edges) deserves a structured decoder, not a flat regressor. Encoder = self-attention over conv tokens (ppm-positioned). Decoder = 8 learned spin-group QUERIES cross-attend the spectrum (DETR-style set prediction). Heads = node (δ, n) + symmetric edge (J, presence). Result: shift 7.5× better (0.037 ppm), J 0.59 Hz, F1 0.94, deg 0.95. Support-region tokens were tested but added nothing — dropped.
Model · two refinements

Polishing the last failure modes — and checking we didn't memorize.

  • Peak-channel input: annotate the spectrum with peak-pick information to sharpen shift localization.
  • Soft-equivalence flag: a learnable edge label marking two groups that share a shift but differ in coupling. When flagged, their shifts are averaged — so an accidentally-degenerate pair renders as one peak, not a spurious doublet.
  • The flag solved the visual artifact; shift MAE matched its predecessor. Both are our production models.

Scaling & roadmap · where this goes

From 64k ChEMBL to 3M+ PubChem — and beyond fixed N.

Now
  • Every model above was trained & compared on the 64k ChEMBL 8-group set.
  • The data pipeline is now scaled to 3.1M PubChem 8-group systems → spectra.
  • Scaled-up production models (more data → larger networks) are training on it now.
The long game
  • Train a family of fixed-N spin-group models (N = 2, 3, … groups) on PubChem.
  • Train a classifier that reads the number of spin groups directly from a spectrum.
  • Compose them → spectrum → spin system for arbitrary small molecules, not just 8-group ones.
The endgame: any small-molecule ¹H spectrum, from any field — decoded into a complete structural answer.
Lifting the fixed-N restriction is the path to generality: a classifier picks N, the matching fixed-N model recovers the spin system. Pipeline already produces 3.1M PubChem systems; scaled production models are training now.
Conclusion

A blurred low-field spectrum → a complete spin system.

  • What we built: a linear-scaling spin simulator (pyspin, 1500/s), a 3M-molecule data pipeline, and a graph-structured decoder that recovers shifts to 0.037 ppm and couplings to 0.59 Hz.
  • What we learned: low field genuinely encodes the full spin system; predicting it as a graph (not 44 flat numbers) was the key step; and plausible training data is sufficient — no measured NMR required.
  • Why it matters: high-field-quality structure from a ~$50k benchtop — a step toward cheaper, in-situ reaction monitoring for synthesis and small-molecule drug discovery.
